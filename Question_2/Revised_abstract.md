The rising number of retracted publications raises questions about whether more flawed articles are being published or problematic research is being identified and removed more efficiently. We examined 2,047 retracted articles indexed in PubMed, analyzing time-to-retraction, journal impact factor (IF), reasons for retraction, and author patterns.
The overall mean time-to-retraction was 32.91 months. Articles published before 2002 took significantly longer to retract than those published after 2002 (49.82 versus 23.82 months; p < 0.0001). Although this suggests faster retraction processes, recent publications have also had less time for problems to emerge. Higher-IF journals generally retracted articles sooner, but IF explained only 1% of variation in retraction time. Its association was somewhat stronger for misconduct-related retractions, particularly fraud.
Reasons for retraction have also diversified. Plagiarism, first recorded as a retraction reason in 1979, accounted for 9.4% of cases, while duplicate publication became increasingly represented. Retractions increasingly occurred earlier in articles’ life cycles, consistent with changing editorial and institutional practices. Moreover, papers by authors with a single retraction accounted for 63.1% of retractions during 1993–2012, compared with 46% during 1972–1992 (p < 0.001), indicating a shift away from concentration among repeat offenders.
However, these patterns cannot establish whether the underlying prevalence of flawed research has increased, given differences in observation periods and detection. These findings suggest that rising retraction counts reflect both broader identification of publication problems and changing retraction practices, rather than scrutiny of high-profile journals alone. Continued monitoring is essential to protect the integrity of the scientific record.
